Political settlements · working note · October 2026

Proxying Khan's axes from public indicators

Indices for horizontal power, vertical power and the unipolar–multipolar distinction, built from V-Dem and the Growth Lab macro panel from the concepts rather than fitted to PolSett. Agreement with PolSett is reported as a result, and the cases where the two disagree are set out so that their drivers can be inspected.

Institutional complexity project · data built · 179 countries, 1960–2023

Summary

  • Within each lens the indicators agree with one another. Across lenses of the same concept they do not, and the pattern suggests substitution: regimes with no formal veto players usually have an informal body able to remove the leader. Horizontal power and unipolarity are therefore scored by their weakest channel.
  • The horizontal index is close to independent of electoral democracy (r = −0.15), which none of the first-pass power proxies achieved.
  • Agreement with PolSett is modest: r ≈ 0.31 on both axes, against reliability ceilings of 0.69 and 0.82. The Khan typology matches in roughly 40% of country-periods, against 36% for the modal class.
  • The vertical index combines two different ideas. Personal command tracks one leader's dominance of the coalition (PolSett's vertical, and the unipolar idea). Party discipline is closer to Khan's vertical dimension.
  • The largest disagreements (Ethiopia, Vietnam, Uganda, Thailand) have identifiable drivers, which can be examined in the case explorer below.

From concept to indicator

Each concept is split into lenses, meaning distinct channels through which it should be observable. A lens is the equal-weight mean of z-scored indicators whose signs were fixed in advance from the concept. The z-scores are anchored on the global country-year panel for 1960–2019, so an index value of zero means the world's average country-year, not the PolSett sample average. No weight or sign was chosen by fit to PolSett.

Khan's vertical dimension is the leadership's power over lower-level factions within its own coalition. It is distinct from the power of elites over non-elites in society. That second reading was also built (from wealth-based power and civil-society participation and consultation), but it correlates −0.84 with electoral democracy and adds nothing beyond it, so it is not carried further here.

Lenses substitute for one another

If the lenses were parallel measures of one latent quantity, they would correlate positively. They do not. Electoral dominance is negatively related to weak social opposition, so hegemonic electoral regimes tend to face large opposition constituencies. Formal unipolarity is negatively related to executive unipolarity. Where courts and legislatures do not constrain the leader, a military or ruling-party body that could remove the leader is usually present.

This is what one would expect if these were formative concepts with substitutable channels. Excluded factions need only one route to holding power, and authority is divided if any one channel divides it. Horizontal power and unipolarity are therefore aggregated as the minimum across lenses. The party and personal lenses of vertical power are averaged, since both are observations of the top's command.

Is it more than democracy?

The first pass failed largely because every V-Dem indicator that predicted a power concept turned out to be a liberties or openness measure. The test here is the correlation with V-Dem's electoral democracy index. Individual lenses are strongly related to democracy, in both directions. The horizontal index ends up close to independent of it because its lenses pull against each other. Unipolarity and vertical power retain a moderate democracy component.

Agreement with PolSett

PolSett is a reference point, not ground truth. Its own coders disagree: the reliability of the published horizontal-power value is 0.47, which caps any external correlation at 0.69. Each comparison below is split into a between-country component (country means) and a within-country component (change across a country's periods).

Reading the quadrants

Khan's typology crosses the two dimensions. Horizontal power is high when excluded factions are weak relative to the ruling coalition. Vertical power is high when the leadership can discipline the lower-level factions inside its own coalition. Throughout this page, each axis is split at the global median of our index, and PolSett splits its own axes at its sample means.

    Where the challenge to the coalition comes from

    Under weakest-channel aggregation, the binding lens is itself a description of the settlement: it names the channel through which excluded factions come closest to imposing costs. Among the periods PolSett codes as having a weak opposition, almost half are bound by the armed lens. PolSett's coders appear to discount armed challenges, especially peripheral ones, that our index counts in full. The armed lens has no measure of how close a challenge comes to the centre, and that is a possible refinement.

    Regimes, their supporters and their opponents

    V-Dem's regime survey divides each country's history into regimes, defined as the formal and informal rules for choosing leaders and keeping them in power. For each regime and year, country experts record which of fourteen social groups the regime depends on, which groups want it replaced and could under favourable conditions remove it, how large each set of groups is as a share of adults, and which single group holds each of five roles, among them the group the regime relies on most and the group most able to affect its survival. These items describe the coalition and the excluded factions in terms close to Khan's, and they are available for every country V-Dem covers.

    Opposition here means opposition to the regime, not to the government. In a democracy, voters for the opposition party who accept the rules are not counted, so the opposition groups are anti-system groups. Select a country, then a regime from the top band of Figure 11.     An open decision: where personal command belongs

    The personal-command lens correlates more strongly with PolSett's items on the leader's control of policy-making than with any of its vertical-power items. The party lens tracks the balance between the leader's bloc and the contingent bloc. This suggests the vertical index mixes two concepts: Khan's vertical dimension, where a possibly collective leadership disciplines lower-level factions (closer to party discipline), and the dominance of one leader over the coalition (closer to the unipolar idea). Vietnam and Uganda show the consequence. A collective Leninist leadership scores low on vertical power, and a personally dominant leader of a loosely disciplined movement scores high.

    The proposed revision is to keep party discipline as the vertical index and move personal command into the unipolarity index. It has not been made yet, because it changes what both indices mean.

    Method notes and files

    Two corrections were made on face validity before any comparison with PolSett. First, the macro panel's UCDP variable for internationalised internal conflict is coded by participant, so Germany, the UK and the US scored as conflict states; only internal conflict is kept, together with V-Dem's measure of political violence by non-state actors. Second, the ruling party's seat share was replaced by the seat gap and electoral turnover. Seat share had placed Ghana and Mexico as weak-dominant, because it measures how large the ruling party is rather than whether the opposition can win.

    Election variables are coded only in election years and are carried forward for up to ten years. GWF regime codes are not labelled in the macro dictionary and were checked against known cases. The Chisols personalist dummy is used as the main personalism measure.
